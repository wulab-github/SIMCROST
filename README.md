SIMCROST (A SIMulator of CROss-membrane Signal Transduction)

Here we present a hybrid simulation method, 
call “SIMCROST” that incorporates both spatial diffusions of proteins and the kinetics of their interactions to mimic the process ranging from ligand-receptor interactions on the plasma membrane to the assembly of scaffold proteins in the cytoplasm. 
We tested SIMCROST on a hypothetical system, using currently available experimental evidence of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) as a prototype. 
The main source code of the simulator, RTK_LDmain_Rcis_v2.f, was written in FORTRAN 77.

A coarse-grained model was designed to simulate various cross-membrane signaling systems. The model partitions the cell into various subcellular compartments. Specifically, the plasma membrane is represented as a two-dimensional flat surface that divides the simulation box into two separate three-dimensional spaces. As shown in Figure 1b, the space above the membrane corresponds to the extracellular region, whereas the space below it represents the cytoplasmic region. Given this setup, an initial configuration was generated prior to the simulation. Ligand dimers (red) were randomly distributed within the extracellular region. Receptors (yellow) were positioned on the cell surface, with their three extracellular beads oriented upward and the cytoplasmic bead facing downward. In the cytoplasmic region, scaffold (green) and effector (blue) proteins were randomly mixed. Each of these proteins was modeled by a polymer that consists of four beads. Every two consecutive beads were connected by a flexible linker. The intramolecular conformation of each polymer was randomly generated. The number of each protein type in the system was set according to its corresponding molecular concentration. Different types of proteins in the simulation box are allowed to interact with each other based on the signaling process described in Figure 1a. Based on this initial configuration, the dynamics of the system was evolved by the following diffusion-reaction algorithm.
Specifically, each simulation time step is divided into two stages. In the first stage, all molecules undergo diffusion; in the second, reactions between different molecule types are executed sequentially. Diffusion is modeled using a Brownian dynamics algorithm, which updates the Cartesian coordinates and velocities of molecular beads based on the forces acting on them. To calculate the forces, a spring constant is assigned between consecutive beads within each molecule to represent flexible linkers. When two molecules interact, a spring is also introduced between their corresponding beads to model the intermolecular bond. Additionally, excluded-volume effects are incorporated through a repulsive force applied between all beads in the system. Moreover, receptor diffusion is restricted to the plasma membrane by constraining the transmembrane region, located between the third and fourth beads of each receptor, to remain on the two-dimensional (2D) surface. Finally, the periodic boundary conditions are imposed.  A 2D periodic boundary condition is applied for receptors. In the extracellular region, periodic boundary conditions are imposed along X and Y directions, while in the Z direction, free ligands are not allowed to move below the plasma membrane. On the other hand, any ligand moving beyond the top of the simulation box will be reflected back. Similarly, in the cytoplasmic region, periodic boundary conditions are also imposed along X and Y directions, while in the Z direction, scaffold and effector proteins are not allowed to move above the plasma membrane. On the other hand, any protein moving beyond the bottom of the simulation box will be reflected back. Ligands or cytoplasmic proteins that bind to receptors move together with them as a single complex on the cell surface through their intermolecular bonds.
This dynamic diffusion process is followed by a reaction step, during which the system evaluates, based on predefined rules, whether new intermolecular bonds have formed or whether existing protein complexes have dissociated. Based on insights from previous literature, we established the following rules for signal activation. First, receptor dimerization induced by ligand binding is required to trigger phosphorylation of the receptor’s intracellular domain, enabling its interaction with the scaffold protein S. Second, only after S binds to the receptor does phosphorylation of S occur, allowing it to recruit the effector protein E. Third, ligand binding to the extracellular domain is required to initiate cis association between two receptors. Following these rules, if a new interaction is formed, an intermolecular bond is created to connect the corresponding pair of beads, thereby constraining their motion in subsequent Brownian dynamics steps. Conversely, any existing interaction between beads has a defined probability of dissociating within each simulation time step. Once dissociation occurs, the bond connecting the original complex is removed, allowing all proteins involved to diffuse independently until they encounter other binding partners in later steps. The probabilities of association and dissociation in each time step are determined by the respective reaction rate constants. All association rates were fixed to a value that maintains computational tractability, corresponding to an experimentally measurable kon of approximately 10⁹M⁻¹s⁻¹, which approaches the upper limit of diffusion-controlled reactions. The dissociation rate, koff, was varied to tune the binding affinities of protein–protein interactions across a broad, biologically meaningful range.
After both diffusion and reaction steps are completed within each time step, the system configuration is updated. Through repeated iteration of this process, the kinetics of the system evolves in both Cartesian space and compositional space.
